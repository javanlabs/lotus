How scoring works

Every project gets one number out of 100 — its Lotus score. This page explains where that number comes from, what its colour means, and what actually moves it.

{{template "lotus_card_section_open" (dict "Padding" "18px 20px" "Style" "margin-bottom:14px")}}

The short version

Lotus reads four tools you already use — SonarQube, Trivy, Sentry and GitLab — and turns each one into its own score out of 100. Those combine into a score for each component, and the components combine into the project's Lotus score. Every score, at every level, is a number from 0 to 100; there are no letter grades.

Nothing here is a judgement Lotus invents. Every number traces back to something one of those four tools already reported about your code, and every metric page shows the arithmetic behind its score in one line.

What the colours mean

A colour is read from the number and nothing else — the same two bands for a tool, a component and a project:

  • 80–100Healthy
  • 0–79Needs attention
  • –No score, with the reason beside it: Not set up, No data, No signal, Not scanned, No pipeline data

So a number and its colour can never disagree. When a tool reports a hard failure — a failed quality gate, a critical vulnerability, a failed pipeline — it lowers that tool's score (a cap below 80), and the colour follows the score.

The four tools

Each is scored 0–100 on its own. Each result is rounded once, to the nearest whole number (halves round up), at the end of its formula.

SONARQUBE · CODE QUALITY

Average of test coverage and the worst rating, times the quality gate. SonarQube rates reliability, security and maintainability A (best) to E (worst). Those letters are SonarQube's own, not Lotus grades: the worst of the three becomes points — A 100, B 80, C 60, D 40, E 20 — and is averaged with coverage, skipping whichever is missing. Then the quality gate multiplies the result: passed ×1, warning ×0.75, failed ×0.5.

Coverage 90 · Worst rating A → 100 · Gate passed ×1 = 95
Coverage 90 · Worst rating A → 100 · Gate failed ×0.5 = 48
No amount of coverage lifts a failed gate above 50: the gate is the verdict your own team configured. With neither coverage nor ratings there is no score (No data).
To raise it: get the gate passing first — the SonarQube page lists which gate conditions failed and by how much. Then fix whatever drags the worst rating, then write tests for untested code.

TRIVY · VULNERABILITIES

Starts at 100. Each critical finding costs 20 points, each high costs 5, never below 0. Medium and low findings are counted and shown, but cost nothing on their own.

Caps: any critical finding caps the score at 49. Without one, if your organization's fail-on policy fails the image (by default: any critical or high finding; it can be made stricter), the score is capped at 79.
100 − 1 critical × 20 − 5 high × 5 = 55 · a critical finding caps it at 49 = 49
100 − 0 critical × 20 − 1 high × 5 = 95 · fails the fail-on policy, capped at 79 = 79
An image with no finished scan has no score (Not scanned).
To raise it: criticals first — one of them holds the score under 50 on its own. Most are fixed by bumping a dependency, not by changing your own code.

SENTRY · ERRORS IN PRODUCTION

The only one that isn't a simple count. Lotus adds up how much load your unresolved issues represent, then converts it: score = 100 × e^(−load / 25). No load is 100, and the score falls away as load grows.

Three things decide an issue's share of the load:

  • How Sentry classifies it — escalating counts 5×, regressed 4×, new 2×, ongoing 1×.
  • How often it fires — on a log scale, so 1,000 events is roughly three times one event, not a thousand times.
  • How recently — an issue's weight halves every 7 days it goes unseen.

Load 5.6 from 4 issues → 100 × e^(−5.6/25) = 80
The band is read from the rounded score, so a 79.6 shows as 80 and is healthy. With several environments, the worst one is the score. A service that has never sent Sentry an event has no score (No signal); one that has, with nothing unresolved, scores 100.
To raise it: resolve escalating and regressed issues first. One escalating issue outweighs five ongoing ones.

GITLAB · PIPELINES AND BRANCHES

Pipeline success rate, less the share of stale branches × 20. The success rate is over the default branch's recent pipelines. A branch is stale when nothing has been committed to it within the staleness window, and stale branches cost at most 20 points — all of them stale.

Cap: if the last finished pipeline failed, the score is capped at 49. A pipeline still running or pending is not a failure; the cap reads the newest one that finished.
Pipelines 85% · 3 of 10 branches stale −6 = 79
Pipelines 90% · no stale branches = 90 · last finished pipeline failed, capped at 49 = 49
A repository with no pipelines has no score (No pipeline data) — branch hygiene alone is not a GitLab score.
To raise it: fix what makes pipelines fail, then delete or merge branches nobody has touched in weeks.

From four scores to one

A component's score is the average of the tools that have a score for it, rounded once. A component with only SonarQube and GitLab is averaged over those two — not penalised for the two it doesn't have. There is no cap at this level: a tool's hard failure has already lowered that tool's own score, and that tool still shows red in its own cell.

A project's Lotus score takes one extra step. Lotus first averages each tool across every component that has a score for it — all your SonarQube scores together, all your Trivy scores together — and then averages those per-tool results. Nothing is rounded until the very end; the project's score is rounded once. If any component has failed (score below 80), the project's score is capped at the lowest component's score: a project cannot be healthy while one of its components is failing. Its colour and word are its band.

WHY THE EXTRA STEP

Say a project has ten components wired to GitLab but only one SonarQube key. Averaging everything in one go would let GitLab outvote SonarQube ten to one — and adding an eleventh component would shift the project's health without anything about its quality changing. Averaging per tool first gives each tool you use an equal say, however many components report it.

An average can hide a problem, so every project verdict carries a count: N of M components need attention — the components with at least one tool below 80. A healthy project can still have a component that needs attention; the count says so.

NO DATA IS NEVER ZERO

A tool with no score — not set up, no data, no signal, not scanned, no pipeline data — is left out of every average, at every level. It is never counted as zero, and never as a pass. Connecting a tool can therefore move a score either way: it starts measuring something that was invisible before.

A worked example

An imaginary project, Storefront, with three components. Each tool's score is the one its formula above produces — payment-api's SonarQube is the failed-gate 48, its Trivy the capped 49, its Sentry the 80, its GitLab the 79. Everything Lotus shows comes out of this one grid, read in two directions.

COMPONENT SONARQUBE TRIVY SENTRY GITLAB ITS SCORE
payment-api 48 49 80 79 64
web-app 95 100 – 98 98
worker – 100 96 – 98
PER TOOL 71.5 83 88 88.5

A dash means that tool has no score for that component — not set up, no signal, no pipeline data. It is skipped, never counted as zero.

READ ACROSS A ROW → A COMPONENT’S SCORE

Average the tools that have a score, round once.
payment-api: (48 + 49 + 80 + 79) ÷ 4 = 64 · Needs attention
web-app: (95 + 100 + 98) ÷ 3 = 97.67 → 98 · Healthy  — Sentry has no signal, so it divides by 3
worker: (100 + 96) ÷ 2 = 98 · Healthy

READ DOWN THE COLUMNS ↓ THE PROJECT’S LOTUS SCORE

First average each tool across the components that have a score for it, without rounding:
SonarQube: (48 + 95) ÷ 2 = 71.5
Trivy: (49 + 100 + 100) ÷ 3 = 83
Sentry: (80 + 96) ÷ 2 = 88
GitLab: (79 + 98) ÷ 2 = 88.5
Then average those four and round once:
(71.5 + 83 + 88 + 88.5) ÷ 4 = 82.75 → 83
Because payment-api failed (64), the project score is capped at the lowest component score:
capped at 64 → 64 · Needs attention  ← Storefront’s Lotus score
The project's tool tabs show those per-tool averages rounded for display — SonarQube 72, Trivy 83, Sentry 88, GitLab 89 — each with how many components are below 80.

A FAILING COMPONENT CAPS THE PROJECT

Storefront's per-tool average is 83, but its Lotus score reads 64 · Needs attention, and its verdict says 1 of 3 components need attention: payment-api has three tools below 80 — a failed quality gate, a critical vulnerability, a GitLab score one point short. A project is only as healthy as its weakest component: if any component fails (< 80), the project's Lotus score is capped at the lowest component score (64).

Note too that the un-capped Lotus score is not the average of the component scores — that would be (64 + 98 + 98) ÷ 3 = 87. Per-tool averaging gives SonarQube's two measurements a full quarter of the project, instead of letting components that don't run SonarQube dilute them. Once payment-api passes, Storefront unblocks to its full per-tool score.

Where to start

  1. Anything capped. A failed quality gate, a critical vulnerability or a failed pipeline holds that tool under 50 on its own. These are usually also the cheapest to fix.
  2. Critical vulnerabilities. Usually one dependency bump away.
  3. Escalating Sentry issues. Weighted 5× and getting worse while you read this.
  4. Coverage on untested files. The slowest to move, but the one that stays moved.
